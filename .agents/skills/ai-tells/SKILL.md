# AI tells

Write so the reader can understand the claim, check its evidence, and act on it. Use this guide while drafting and for a
separate editing pass afterward. These are editing cues, not a test of who wrote a passage. A word or punctuation mark
alone is no reason to rewrite a clear sentence.

## Keep the meaning

Preserve requirements, exceptions, uncertainty, and approval boundaries. Do not turn "may" into "must" or remove a
warning merely to shorten the text. Keep technical terms consistent, and preserve code, commands, paths, identifiers,
quotes, and format examples. Do not invent evidence to make a sentence sound more confident.

Follow the project's terminology and any required template. Use an author's writing samples when matching their voice
matters. Do not import company names, internal systems, ticket numbers, or private examples from another project.

## Claims and evidence

Replace inflated significance with the event or result: "marks a pivotal moment" usually says less than what actually
changed. Name the source behind "experts believe" or remove the attribution. Keep a limitation when it affects what the
evidence supports.

Remove unsupported percentages, universal claims, and predictions. Keep measured results with their scope and source.
Name a problem and its effect instead of writing "despite challenges". End with an actual decision, result, or next step
when one is needed.

## Sentence patterns

State the point directly. Replace "it's not just X, it's Y" with the claim about Y unless the distinction helps the
reader. Remove trailing reassurance such as "no guessing" when the preceding sentence already explains the behavior.

Use as many examples as the explanation needs. Avoid padding lists to three items, cycling through synonyms for one
concept, or describing unrelated topics as a range. Replace "showcasing" or "reflecting" with the specific relationship.
Use "is" when "serves as" adds no meaning.

Fragments are useful in labels and checklists. Use complete sentences when the actor, action, or condition would
otherwise be unclear.

## Word choice

Prefer concrete nouns and ordinary verbs. Replace figurative uses of "landscape", "tapestry", "journey", and "realm"
with the subject. Replace "leverage" with "use", "in order to" with "to", and "due to the fact that" with "because" when
the meaning stays the same.

Keep literal and technical meanings: a navigation control navigates, and an optimizer optimizes. Keep necessary
uncertainty, but remove stacked hedges such as "could potentially possibly". Avoid slogans and metaphors when a direct
instruction explains the task.

## Punctuation and layout

Use sentence case for headings, preserving acronyms and proper names. Use headings to organize sections, lists for
parallel items or steps, and tables for comparisons. Keep required labels in templates and report schemas.

Reserve bold for terms or details a reader needs to find. Remove decorative bold, emojis, and headings whose next
sentence merely repeats them. Prefer periods or commas when an em dash interrupts a sentence unnecessarily. Keep hyphens
that clarify grammar and straight quotes in technical examples.

Remove chatbot greetings, praise, offers to continue, and announcements such as "let's dive in" from standalone
artifacts. Begin with the content.

## Editing pass

1. Read the whole passage for its purpose, audience, and technical meaning.
1. Check claims for evidence and scope. Cut filler and specify vague nouns.
1. Check sentence patterns and layout. Rewrite a repetitive passage as a whole when local substitutions leave it
   awkward.
1. Compare the edit with the original. Confirm that requirements, exceptions, examples, links, and uncertainty still
   mean the same thing.

Leave clear prose alone. Report material changes and any claim that still needs verification; do not claim that an edit
proves human authorship.
